A look into ethic: How Companies Learn Your Secrets

Introduction

Charles Duhigg published an article on February 16, 2012 in The New York Times showing how Target used a customer’s shopping habits to predict whether or not someone was pregnant. They do this by collection information about the customers’ purchases and used statistical models to see whether their purchases matches patterns associated with pregnancy. This would then give them a lead in advertising as they could send them advertisements before any other company. Even though this helped Target’s marketing, this act also raised ethical questions about privacy.

Ethical dilemma

According to Duhigg (2012), Target uses a unique identification number for each customer that allowed the company to track their purchases over time. Andrew Pole, a statistician at Target, analyzed purchasing patterns from women who had registered for baby showed, and identified approximately 25 products that could be used to identify whether a customer was pregnant or not. Target then used these predictions to send advertisements for baby-related products.

The ethical dilemma is whether or not Target should be using purchasing information to predict sensitive customer informations, without the customers explicitly agreeing to that use. While collecting purchasing information for marketing purposes is common, using it to infer about someone’s pregnancy could be going beyond the bounds of what many customers would expect.

Data collection process

Target collected customer information through purchases, credit card transactions, surveys, coupons, and various other other company interactions then linked to each customer through a unique identification number (Duhigg 2012). Target initially analyzed the purchasing habits of women who had voluntarily registered for baby showers to find products associated with pregnancy. Although the information was collected through ethical means, using it to predict sensitive personal information raises ethical concerns, especially when customers were not explicitly informed about these predictions. Their data collection process wasn’t perfect, however. Customers who paid with cash or made purchases that could not be linked to their identification number might have had incomplete purchasing histories.

Informed consent

One ethical issue with this is the lack of any informed consent. Target did not formally recruit customers to participate in a pregnancy prediction analysis, but rather they came to these conclusions based on information it had already collected. Most customers were likely unaware of the fact that their purchasing histories could be used to predict sensitive information, such as pregnancy. Target could have obtained informed consent from its customers by clearly explaining how their information would be analyzed and that sensitive information could potentially be inferred from their data. However, customers cannot meaningfully consent to applications of their data that are not yet foreseen. They can agree to broad categories of data use, but this does not necessarily mean they understand or agree to the potential consequences. Informed consent becomes difficult when customers do not know what conclusions could be drawn from their data. In the case of Target, customers might have agreed to data collection for marketing without realizeing that their purchases could be used to predict sensitive information about their personal lives.

Does this protect the privacy and security of individuals represented in our data?

Target’s data collection and inference process demonstrates the opposite. Although the customer’s information was collected through ordinary shopping transactions, analyzing this information sometimes lead to conclusions that can compromise the privacy of an individual. For example, Duhigg (2012) described an incident where a teenage girl’s father discovered her pregnancy after Target sent her advertisements for baby products. Even though Target did not directly disclose the information, its advertising process unintentionally revealed something private about her to her family. This demonstrates that companies must also consider how the conclusions drawn from their data could expose sensitive information about individuals.

Does this improve life for their customers?

Overall, Target’s usage of customer data benefited themselves more than its customers. Its analysis of customer purchasing patterns allowed Target to reach customers at a time when their shopping habits were more likely to change, potentially increasing sales. Customers could also benefit from reciving coupons and advertisements for the products they might need, but at the cost of their privacy. While the data certainly helped Target improve its business, it’s questionable whether it improved customers’ lives when their information were being analyzed in ways they did not expect.

Conclusion

Target’s use of customer data demonstrates how data science can be used to benefit companies while raising ethical concerns for their customers. By analyzing purchasing patterns, Target was able to predict sensitive information and use that information to increase sales. While customers might have benefited from receiving relevant advertisements and coupons, they had little control over how their information was being used and faced potential privacy risks. In its pursuit of greater profits, Target demonstrated the power it had over its customers through data collection and analysis. This example shows the importance of informed consent and protecting the privacy and security of individuals, especially when personal information is used in ways that customers may not expect.
